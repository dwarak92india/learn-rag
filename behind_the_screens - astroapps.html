It's 11:47 PM.

You've checked your phone seventeen times. Your ex hasn't replied. Your friends have stopped entertaining your theories, and even your Spotify algorithm seems to be recommending breakup songs.

Then you remember an app that promises access to people who might have some answers.

You open it.

Birth date. Birth time. Birthplace.

Apparently, the universe needs your KYC before it can comment on your love life.

Three screens later, you're staring at a marketplace of astrologers. One has 15 years of experience. Another specialises in relationships. A third has thousands of glowing reviews.

And then you spot the two most beautiful words in consumer technology.

FREE CHAT.

What follows is an interesting intersection of human psychology, marketplace design and the economics of selling a service whose outcome is inherently uncertain.

Welcome to Behind the Screens, where we examine the invisible behavioural forces operating inside everyday products.

Today's subject: astrology marketplaces.

Not the planets. Not the bad boys of astrology—Rahu, Ketu and Saturn. But the product architecture that connects a person looking for answers with an expert offering interpretations—and the behavioural mechanisms that can bring that person back.

Because while the stars may be unpredictable, the user journey is very much designed.

01The psychology · Goal completion and the Hook Model

The free consultation: Congratulations, you've unlocked a new question.

Our protagonist—let's call him Rahul—has one question.

"Will she come back?"

He clicks the free consultation, shares his birth details and begins chatting.

The astrologer studies his chart. A few messages arrive. There's a planetary explanation, a reference to Venus and perhaps a suggestion that the coming months could be significant.

Rahul leans forward.

Finally, some answers.

And just as things are getting interesting...

Rahul came looking for closure. He now has a cliffhanger.

What's happening behind the screen?

Nir Eyal's Hooked Model describes four components of habit-forming products: Trigger, Action, Variable Reward and Investment. Here, Rahul's relationship anxiety is the internal trigger, and the free consultation is the action that lets him experience the service with minimal commitment. [1]

There's another relevant idea: our tendency to resume interrupted activities.

The Ovsiankina effect describes this tendency, and a 2025 meta-analysis found evidence for it across the studies examined. That doesn't mean every unfinished conversation will compel someone to pay. But when Rahul's original goal remains unresolved, the option to continue has an obvious appeal. [2]

For the PM, the free consultation is an activation mechanism. It lets users sample a service that would otherwise require trust in an unfamiliar expert.

The interesting design decision isn't simply offering something free. It's how much value users receive before the trial ends.

Does Rahul leave knowing whether the service is worth purchasing? Or does he need to purchase the service to find out?

Either way, he's now considering his first transaction.

And that's where the wallet enters the story.

02The psychology · Transaction decoupling

The wallet: You came for three minutes. Your money booked a longer stay.

Rahul decides to continue.

He finds an astrologer charging ₹20 per minute. He estimates that three minutes should be enough to get a reasonably clear answer.

That's ₹60.

Simple mathematics. Even Mercury retrograde can't interfere with this one.

Except Rahul may not be purchasing three minutes directly.

In a wallet-based marketplace, he might first be asked to recharge a minimum of ₹100.

He pays, starts his consultation and spends ₹60. The remaining ₹40 sits in his wallet.

Now something subtle has changed.

Before recharging, another consultation required Rahul to spend more money. After recharging, he can use money he's already deposited.

His next consultation may feel like a smaller financial decision, even though the money remains economically valuable.

What's happening behind the screen?

Consumer researchers Dilip Soman and John Gourville have studied transaction decoupling, where payment and consumption are separated. Their work demonstrates that the way payments are structured can influence the psychological connection between paying and consuming. [3]

A prepaid wallet introduces this separation into the consultation journey.

For the product, the benefits are straightforward: faster checkout, fewer payment interruptions and a smoother path between experts.

For the user, it can be convenient, particularly during an ongoing conversation.

But there's an interesting hypothesis here. Does the presence of unused wallet balance make Rahul more willing to book another consultation than he would be if he had to pay afresh?

That's a question for an experiment, not an assumption.

Compare exact-amount payments with prepaid wallets. Examine consultation frequency, unused balances, spending and satisfaction.

Because there's a meaningful difference between making a purchase easier and making the cost of that purchase less noticeable.

Rahul, meanwhile, has successfully recharged his wallet.

His consultation begins.

And a clock starts ticking.

The ticking clock: Your emotional crisis has a billing cycle.

Rahul has five minutes with his chosen astrologer.

Five minutes to explain a three-year relationship, two breakups, one suspicious Instagram story and the complicated matter of who blocked whom first.

Rahul has two competing objectives: explain his situation properly and receive an interpretation before the session ends.

The astrologer has their own challenge: understand enough context to offer useful guidance within the purchased time.

And the timer is an unusually effective third participant in this conversation.

Unlike Rahul's ex, it communicates consistently.

What's happening behind the screen?

Time pressure can change how people process information. Research on consumer decision-making suggests that time-constrained users may simplify their search and prioritise information that's easier to evaluate. [4]

A consultation timer is not necessarily an artificial scarcity tactic. When the service is billed by the minute, the time limit is real, and displaying it provides valuable cost transparency.

But the UX implementation matters.

A countdown occupying a prominent position in the chat can make the remaining time more salient. For someone discussing an emotionally complex problem, this may compete with the attention required to articulate their concern.

And what happens if the session ends halfway through an explanation?

The product decision

The first path supports uninterrupted service consumption. The second helps users evaluate what they've already received.

Both can be legitimate. Their prominence, however, can influence the next action.

For a PM, I'd investigate whether collecting the user's main question before the paid timer begins improves consultation quality. I'd also test whether a post-session summary reduces unnecessary extensions without reducing satisfaction.

Because a user purchasing five minutes of consultation presumably wants five minutes of useful consultation—not three minutes explaining why their ex is emotionally unavailable.

And if Rahul still feels that he hasn't received a sufficiently clear answer, the marketplace offers another option.

Another astrologer.

04The psychology · Reassurance-seeking and choice architecture

The second opinion: Three experts, four predictions and absolutely no peace.

Rahul's first astrologer suggests there's a reasonable possibility of reconciliation.

He's delighted.

Then he remembers something.

What if the astrologer missed an important planetary position?

A second opinion seems sensible.

The marketplace has hundreds of profiles, ratings, specialisations and price points. Rahul finds another expert who specialises in relationship astrology.

The second astrologer has a different interpretation.

"Focus on your future. This relationship may have run its course."

Oh.

Rahul now has two opinions and considerably less clarity than when he started.

Naturally, he considers consulting a third astrologer.

At this point, his relationship has become a distributed consensus problem, except none of the nodes agree.

What's happening behind the screen?

Marketplaces are built around choice. More experts, better filters, visible ratings and transparent pricing can help users find a suitable service provider.

But when the service involves interpreting an uncertain personal outcome, more choice also means more opportunities to seek alternative answers.

There are two relevant behavioural concepts here.

The first is choice architecture. Research on choice overload suggests that additional options don't necessarily make decisions worse; their effects depend on decision complexity, preference uncertainty and the difficulty of comparing alternatives. [5]

Rahul isn't simply choosing between three experts. He's deciding how much confidence to place in three potentially contradictory interpretations.

The second is reassurance-seeking.

Research by Parrish and Radomsky examines how repeated requests for reassurance can arise from emotionally significant concerns. Although their clinical findings cannot be directly generalised to ordinary astrology users, they provide a useful framework for investigating repeated consultations motivated by unresolved uncertainty. [6]

Consider two hypothetical user journeys.

Journey A · The resolved user

Rahul consults an expert, receives an interpretation, reflects on it and leaves feeling that the conversation was useful.

One consultation. A satisfactory experience.
Journey B · The uncertain user

Rahul consults three experts, receives conflicting interpretations and continues looking for a more definitive answer.

Three consultations. An unresolved question.

Two hypothetical journeys that illustrate why consultation volume alone cannot establish user value.

From a conventional revenue dashboard, Journey B might look attractive. More sessions, more minutes purchased, more transactions.

But is Rahul three times as satisfied?

We have absolutely no idea.

And that's the interesting measurement problem.

A repeat consultation might indicate trust in the marketplace, curiosity about different perspectives or dissatisfaction with previous answers. It might also reflect an ongoing emotional need that no single consultation can resolve.

Without understanding the user's motivation, a PM risks interpreting all repeat behaviour as successful retention.

Now imagine that the third astrologer tells Rahul exactly what he hoped to hear.

"There's a strong possibility she'll return."

Suddenly, the universe is back in his good books.

Which brings us to perhaps the most interesting part of the journey.

05The psychology · Variable rewards and internal triggers

The variable reward: Hope has excellent retention potential.

Rahul finally receives an encouraging prediction.

His mood improves. He closes the app, orders dinner and starts mentally drafting the speech he'll give when his ex inevitably returns.

For the next few days, life is good.

But then Friday arrives.

No message.

Saturday.

Still nothing.

By Sunday, Rahul has investigated her last-seen status, analysed an Instagram caption and consulted two mutual friends who would very much like to be excluded from this investigation.

The original uncertainty has returned.

And so has the possibility of opening the app.

What's happening behind the screen?

This is where Nir Eyal's Hook Model becomes particularly interesting.

Eyal describes variable rewards as an important component of habit-forming experiences. When the outcome of an action is not entirely predictable, anticipation can motivate continued engagement. [1]

In an astrology marketplace, the consultation outcome is naturally variable. Different experts may provide different interpretations, and the user's circumstances may change over time.

But there's an important distinction.

A variable outcome doesn't automatically create a variable-reward loop. Nor should every moment of reassurance be described as a dopamine hit. That would be a convenient explanation, but an unnecessarily simplistic one.

The stronger product hypothesis is that temporary reassurance and persistent uncertainty can coexist.

Rahul might feel better immediately after a consultation without gaining any new certainty about what will actually happen.

When his circumstances fail to change, the original concern can become an internal trigger again.

And because he already knows how the marketplace works, has saved his birth details and perhaps has money left in his wallet, the path back is relatively short.

The particularly interesting part is the investment stage.

Every completed consultation can potentially make the next one easier. Rahul knows which experts he prefers. He understands the pricing. His birth details are saved. He no longer needs to overcome the uncertainty of using an unfamiliar product.

A marketplace may also recommend experts based on previous consultations or surface relevant specialisations.

All of these features can improve the experience.

But they also mean that when Rahul feels the urge to consult again, there's very little standing between the urge and the transaction.

The key product question is whether repeated use corresponds to increasing perceived value—or simply reflects the persistence of the problem that brought him there in the first place.

And that's where this story gets bigger than astrology.

06The product paradox

What if your happiest customer doesn't come back?

Rahul's journey exposes a fascinating tension in consumer product design.

Most subscription businesses and marketplaces spend considerable effort improving retention. They want customers to return, transact frequently and build long-term relationships with the product.

Fair enough. Investors aren't generally thrilled by a dashboard announcing that everyone has left.

But consider a product whose users arrive with an unresolved emotional concern.

If the product genuinely helps Rahul gain perspective, he may no longer feel the need to consult another expert about the same question.

His immediate retention could decline precisely because he received value.

Meanwhile, another user might return repeatedly because their concern remains unresolved.

That user could generate more revenue despite experiencing less clarity.

This doesn't mean repeat consultations are inherently problematic. Users can enjoy astrology as entertainment, value ongoing guidance or genuinely prefer hearing several perspectives.

The point is that identical product events can have entirely different psychological motivations.

A PM who measures only what users do may miss why they're doing it.

And when the product serves an emotional need, that distinction becomes especially important.

07The PM's Dharma

A little guidance from the 9th house

Every PM has a little Saturn in their roadmap—deadlines, revenue targets and the occasional existential crisis over retention metrics.

But perhaps there's some inspiration to borrow from the 9th house, traditionally associated with dharma, wisdom and good fortune.

What if we looked at a few ways to bring user value and business growth a little closer together?

Rather than questioning the existing product mechanics, here are four experiments that might be interesting to explore.

Experiment 01

Can the free trial have a more satisfying ending?

What if the introductory session focused on answering one clearly defined question rather than offering a fixed number of minutes?

Worth measuring: Perceived value, trial-to-paid conversion and post-trial satisfaction.

Experiment 02

A little less explaining, a little more consulting.

Could allowing users to submit their primary question and context before the paid timer starts make consultations more meaningful?

Worth measuring: Consultation satisfaction, effective conversation time and session extensions.

Experiment 03

Something to take home.

Would a simple post-consultation summary help users reflect on the conversation and revisit the guidance without needing another session?

Worth measuring: Perceived clarity, repeat consultations about the same concern and satisfaction.

Experiment 04

Does retention always mean satisfaction?

It might be interesting to understand why users return. Is it a new question, a follow-up with a trusted expert or an attempt to find clarity on an unresolved concern?

Worth measuring: Repeat consultation intent, resolution by user cohort and long-term satisfaction.

These are just a few hypotheses worth exploring. The objective isn't necessarily to reduce consultation frequency, but to better understand which interactions create value and which ones signal an unmet need.

Perhaps there's an opportunity to make consultations more meaningful while building a healthier long-term relationship between users, experts and the marketplace.

And who knows? Maybe there's a sweet spot where the user's peace of mind and the product's growth chart are both looking up. Even Saturn might approve.
✦The final prediction

It's been two weeks.

Rahul's ex still hasn't replied. He's consulted three astrologers, has ₹40 remaining in his wallet and now knows considerably more about Venus than he ever intended.

But he has also discovered something about the product.

The next time he has an unresolved question, he knows exactly where to go.

And that is the fascinating thing about habit-forming products. Their strongest triggers don't always live inside the interface.

Sometimes, they live in the unanswered questions users carry around with them.

The marketplace simply makes returning incredibly easy.

The real product question isn't how many times Rahul comes back. It's whether each visit leaves him better off than the last.

As for whether his ex will return?

That's outside the scope of this product study.

But somewhere, an astrologer is online.
